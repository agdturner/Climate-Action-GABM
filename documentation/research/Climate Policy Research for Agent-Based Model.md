# **Ideological Polarization in United Kingdom Climate Policy: Empirical Parameterization for Agent-Based Modeling**

## **1\. Introduction: The Fracture of the Climate Consensus**

The simulation of public opinion dynamics regarding climate change policy in the United Kingdom requires highly calibrated, empirically grounded parameters that accurately reflect the current ideological environment. Following the July 2024 General Election and subsequent political realignments into early 2026, the traditional cross-party consensus on achieving "Net Zero" by 2050 has fractured significantly. To effectively populate an academic agent-based model (ABM) with authentic ideological framing, one must map the extreme bounds of the political spectrum. In the contemporary British context, these opposing poles are anchored by the Green Party of England and Wales (GPEW) on the progressive left and Reform UK on the populist right.1

Relying on Large Language Models (LLMs) to generate synthetic political messaging for ABMs frequently results in rhetoric that gravitates toward a generic, centrist mean, failing to capture the visceral, high-valence language utilized by political actors operating at the ideological fringes. Reform UK operates on a platform explicitly characterized as "anti-Net Zero populism".4 The party systematically decouples environmental policy from ecological science, instead reframing climate targets as elite, "nanny state" overreach that actively impoverishes the working class through higher energy bills, deindustrialization, and increased taxation.6 Conversely, the Green Party of England and Wales utilizes a strategy characterized by political scientists as "eco-populism".3 Under the leadership of Carla Denyer and Adrian Ramsay, the party explicitly links climate mitigation with anti-austerity wealth redistribution, framing the fossil fuel industry as an exploitative elite extracting wealth from the public and destroying the biosphere.9

This report provides an exhaustive analysis of these opposing ideologies across six specific climate policies. It establishes the empirical foundations required to replace synthetic ABM inputs with real, attributable, sourced public statements, ensuring high-fidelity ideological clusters within the simulated network.

## **2\. Methodological Framework for Simulation Input**

Integrating empirical political rhetoric into an agent-based model requires translating qualitative statements into quantitative vectors. The broadcast-ready messages synthesized in this report are designed to trigger specific attribute responses within simulated agents. Reform UK’s messaging is calibrated to activate parameters related to economic anxiety, national sovereignty, and anti-regulatory sentiment.11 Their rhetoric consistently emphasizes the immediate financial burden of green transitions, leveraging the post-2022 energy price shocks to frame decarbonization as an unaffordable luxury.2

The Green Party’s rhetoric, by contrast, activates parameters related to environmental salience, global empathy, and state-interventionist preferences. Their messaging demands unprecedented state investment, funded by aggressive wealth and corporate taxation, to subsidize the public’s transition to zero-carbon technologies.14 Notably, analyzing these extremes reveals underlying meta-stances. For example, Reform UK frequently rejects the fundamental premise of climate policies—such as international compensation or housing mandates—viewing the questions themselves as invalid constructs imposed by supranational bodies or out-of-touch bureaucrats.12 Similarly, the Green Party’s internal policy evolution demonstrates a shift from revenue-neutral models (like the carbon fee and dividend) toward state-directed capital expenditure, prioritizing structural upgrades over individual cash redistribution.17

### **4.1. Renewable Energy Infrastructure**

The acceleration of renewable energy infrastructure represents a fundamental fault line in UK political economy. Reform UK’s rhetorical strategy relies on framing renewable energy—specifically onshore and offshore wind and solar farms—not as a technological advancement, but as a heavily subsidized financial burden imposed on British taxpayers by a globalist elite.7 The party categorically opposes the further roll-out of these technologies. In parliamentary motions and public broadcasts throughout 2024 and 2025, Deputy Leader Richard Tice and Leader Nigel Farage have consistently argued that intermittent renewable energy requires expensive grid upgrades and backup gas generation, thereby driving up household electricity bills.19

For an agent-based model, a Reform UK broadcast message in this domain should trigger high economic anxiety parameters among simulated voters, framing "Net Zero" as a wealth-destroying ideology rather than an environmental necessity.2 Conversely, the Green Party of England and Wales places the rapid acceleration of renewable energy at the absolute center of its macroeconomic policy.1 Co-leaders Carla Denyer and Adrian Ramsay frame the transition to wind and solar power not only as a climate necessity but as the primary mechanism for reducing the cost of living and generating high-quality employment.23 The party's 2024 manifesto proposes massive state intervention, pledging £40 billion annually toward green economic transformation, with £50 billion specifically targeted over the parliament for electricity generation and storage.15 In opinion dynamics modeling, Green Party messaging should appeal to agents with high baseline environmental concern and those receptive to arguments that state-funded infrastructure projects can simultaneously solve economic and ecological crises.

### **4.2. Hydrocarbon Extraction and Energy Security**

The debate over the extraction of domestic fossil fuels reveals deeply entrenched narratives regarding national security, economic independence, and global environmental responsibility. Reform UK vehemently opposes any ban on the extraction of domestic fossil fuels, viewing the continued exploitation of the North Sea as a patriotic imperative.11 In the wake of global geopolitical instability—such as conflicts in the Middle East and the war in Ukraine—Reform UK figures argue that refusing to drill domestically forces the UK to rely on expensive, carbon-intensive imports.24 They characterize bans on new licenses as an act of "economic self-harm" that destroys highly skilled jobs and forfeits billions in potential tax revenue.24 When simulating Reform UK messaging in this domain, the primary rhetorical vectors are patriotism, job protection, and anti-globalism. The rhetoric is designed to resonate with working-class agents in post-industrial or coastal regions where the energy sector provides primary employment.

In stark contrast, the Green Party's opposition to fossil fuel extraction is absolute and uncompromising. For the Greens, approving new oil and gas licenses—such as the controversial Rosebank field in the North Sea—is viewed as a direct betrayal of future generations and a violation of global climate limits.26 The party's parliamentary representatives frequently leverage their platform in the House of Commons to demand the immediate cessation of all new extraction projects.28 They reject the economic arguments for continued drilling, pointing out that North Sea oil is sold on international wholesale markets and therefore does not lower domestic consumer bills.13 In an ABM, Green messaging on this issue serves as a high-valence purity test, appealing strongly to climate-anxious voter agents who demand absolute adherence to scientific emissions targets and view the fossil fuel industry as inherently destructive.

### **4.3. The Automotive Transition**

The transition away from internal combustion engine (ICE) vehicles encapsulates the tension between individual consumer freedom and collective environmental mandates. Reform UK aggressively targets the 2030 phase-out of ICE vehicles as a primary example of overbearing state intervention. The party argues that the Zero Emission Vehicle (ZEV) mandate and the ban on new petrol and diesel cars are destroying the British automotive industry and restricting the freedom of the working class, who are currently priced out of the electric vehicle (EV) market.29 Reform UK's messaging consistently frames the EV transition as a "destructive" policy forced upon an unwilling public by out-of-touch metropolitan elites.7 For the ABM, this messaging taps directly into agent parameters related to personal liberty, anti-regulatory sentiment, and immediate cost-of-living anxieties. It positions the car not just as a mode of transport, but as a symbol of autonomy threatened by environmentalism.

Conversely, the Green Party views the 2030 ban not just as necessary, but as insufficiently ambitious given the scale of the climate crisis. In their 2024 manifesto, the party advocated accelerating the ban on the sale of new petrol and diesel vehicles to 2027, and impressively, banning their use entirely on roads by 2035\.15 The party acknowledges the massive financial barrier this poses to ordinary citizens, and thus pairs the ban with demands for an extensive, multi-billion-pound vehicle scrappage scheme and massive investments in active travel and public transport infrastructure.15 In the simulation, this message appeals to agents who prioritize rapid decarbonization and state-subsidized technological transitions, though the extremity of the 2027/2035 timeline may alienate moderate or financially constrained agents within the network.

### **4.4. The Built Environment and Domestic Heating**

The regulation of the built environment sits at the intersection of climate policy, the housing crisis, and domestic comfort. On the issue of housing mandates, Reform UK adopts a meta-stance that rejects the premise of green building regulations altogether. The party views mandates for heat pumps, solar panels, and enhanced insulation as prime examples of "Net Zero" ideology artificially inflating the cost of building new homes during an acute national housing shortage.7 Reform MPs utilize parliamentary debates to highlight the high upfront costs and alleged inefficiencies of heat pumps compared to traditional gas boilers, arguing that the technology is being forced upon a public that cannot afford it.16 In the ABM, this rhetoric is highly effective at swaying simulated voters who possess high housing-cost anxiety, linking the climate agenda directly to the unattainability of homeownership and the micromanagement of private property.

### **4.5. Carbon Pricing Mechanisms**

The economic mechanism of carbon pricing serves as a proxy for broader debates on taxation, corporate responsibility, and state intervention. Reform UK categorically opposes the imposition of carbon taxes. Their stated policy is to abolish the UK's existing carbon pricing mechanisms, specifically targeting the UK Emissions Trading Scheme.21 Richard Tice has repeatedly attacked carbon taxes in Parliament, arguing that they cripple domestic industries—specifically refining, steel, and chemical manufacturing—while being passed directly onto consumers via extortionate wholesale electricity prices.21 Because Reform UK entirely rejects the tax itself, they inherently reject the premise of a "fee and dividend" model; in their view, the tax should not exist to be distributed in the first place. In an ABM, this messaging activates anti-taxation and pro-industry parameters among conservative agents, linking abstract climate policy directly to the deindustrialization of the British heartlands.

The Green Party's stance on this specific policy formulation requires careful nuance. While the party historically championed a pure "Carbon Fee and Dividend" model (where revenues are directly returned to citizens' bank accounts to offset consumer price increases), internal debates led to a significant shift in their 2024 manifesto.17 They strongly support the implementation of a rigorous, escalating carbon tax, proposing to set it initially at £120 per tonne and rising over ten years to a maximum of £500 per tonne.34 However, the commitment to distribute the revenue as a direct public *dividend* was explicitly omitted from their 2024 platform; instead, the projected £80 billion in annual revenue is earmarked to fund public services, the NHS, and subsidize the broader green transition.15 Therefore, their stance on the *tax* is "Strongly support," but their stance on the survey's *dividend framing* is mixed/conditional. The ABM modeler must account for this shift from pure redistribution to state-led investment.

### **4.6. International Climate Finance and Global Justice**

The debate over international climate finance—specifically compensating nations in the Global South for "loss and damage" caused by historical emissions—highlights a stark division regarding the UK's global role. Reform UK fundamentally rejects the concept of international climate finance or "climate reparations." The party's ideology is intensely nationalist, skeptical of multilateral institutions, and prioritizes domestic expenditure over international aid.12 Deputy Leader Richard Tice has explicitly called for the UK to quit the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC), labeling it and its associated scientific bodies as deeply flawed and expensive institutions that fail British voters.12 The party's manifesto explicitly pledges to cut the foreign aid budget by 50%.14 For an opinion dynamics model, this rhetoric effectively mobilizes agents with high "in-group" preference and low trust in globalism, framing climate finance as an unjust wealth transfer from struggling domestic taxpayers to foreign governments.

The Green Party operates on a platform of profound internationalism and global climate justice.10 They view the UK—as an early industrializer—as bearing a historical, moral, and economic obligation to compensate nations in the Global South suffering from climate-induced loss and damage.36 In parliamentary debates throughout 2024 and 2025, Green MPs actively lobbied the government to increase contributions to the UN Loss and Damage Fund, explicitly demanding that this finance be provided as non-repayable grants rather than debt-inducing loans.37 They frame the failure to provide adequate finance as a "scandal" that forces the poorest nations to spend more on debt servicing than on healthcare or education.37 In the ABM, this messaging appeals to agents with high global empathy and egalitarian values, framing climate change fundamentally as a crisis of global inequality and historical exploitation.

## **5\. Synthesis and Expected Dynamics in Simulation**

The empirical texts provided in this report illustrate a profound, non-overlapping ideological bifurcation in UK climate politics.
